# A tuple type is keyed by its shape

**Decision.** A witness may be keyed on a tuple type, and the head it keys on is the type's *shape*: the label at each field position, `""` where the field is positional, arity implied by the length, field types excluded. `HeadKey::TupleType(Vec<String>)` (`curios-elab/src/concept.rs`) is that head, read off a weak-head normal form by `HeadKey::of_whnf` in both the first-order position and the higher-kinded one, so `satisfy (@A: Type, @B: Type, use Show(A), use Show(B)) => Show({A, B}) { … }` registers and `satisfy Functor(Pair)` registers for a `let Pair(A: Type) -> Type = {Nat, A};`. Every other rule — regularity, one witness per key, the orphan rule, deferral — applies to a tuple key unchanged, because a shape is one more thing a parameter can be rigidly headed by and nothing else.

**Rationale.** For `List(Nat)` the head is `List` and `Nat` is checked by unification after lookup. A tuple type has no name to be headed by, so the question is what part of it plays that role: rigid, cheap to read off a weak-head form, and exactly the part conversion does not delegate to the components. That part is the shape. `compare_tuple_type` (`curios-elab/src/convert.rs`) refuses two tuple types whose labels differ before it enqueues a single field, and equates them iff the labels agree and the fields convert; `TupleType`'s hand-written `Eq`/`Hash` (`curios-core/src/term/shape.rs`) reassert those labels on top of the label-blind telescope identity for the same reason. A key that reads the labels and leaves the fields to unification therefore splits the type along the seam the language already splits it on. Reading it costs a walk down the spine and evaluates nothing: the weak-head form of a tuple type is the node itself, and `Telescope::labels` opens no binder.

Two consequences follow rather than being decided. `{Nat, Bool}`, `{a: Nat, b: Bool}` and `{x: Nat, y: Bool}` are three keys because they are three types, so a witness for one does not serve another and the miss is reported with a hint naming the rule. And a tuple shape is owned by no module — the answer `mount_of_head` already gives for an intrinsic former — so the orphan rule reads, for a tuple key: declared where the concept is declared, or by a privileged root. That is `List`'s standing today, and it makes `/std`'s arity ceiling a promise rather than a default, which is why the ceiling in `/std/Tuple` was chosen with room.

**Rejected.** *Arity alone.* `{A, B}` and `{x: A, y: B}` would be one key though they are two types, and the one-witness-per-key rule would refuse the second because the first exists — a key coarser than the identity it stands for.

*A single arity-blind `Tuple` head.* One witness per concept would cover every tuple type, but its body cannot be written: no term in the language ranges over a tuple of unknown arity. Only a compiler that writes bodies could fill it, which is derivation and a different objective, and a key nobody can write a witness for buys nothing.

*The component heads in the key* — `{Nat, Bool}` keyed as the pair of `Nat` and `Bool`. Keys would overlap: `Show({Nat, B})` and `Show({A, Bool})` would both register and `Show({Nat, Bool})` would be ambiguous, which is the instance-overlap problem the one-head-per-parameter discipline exists to make unrepresentable. A telescope variable at a position has no head to contribute in any case.

*A nominal product in `/std`* — `Pair(A, B)`, `Triple(A, B, C)` — carrying the witnesses instead. `(1, true)` has type `{Nat, Bool}` and never `Pair(Nat, Bool)`, so every call site would pay a conversion and the language's own product would be the one type its standard library has no opinion about.

*Componentwise resolution wired into the compiler* for concepts known to distribute over products. Which concepts those are is library knowledge; the compiler has no business knowing that `Show` distributes and `Monad` does not.

*The tuple former owned by every root*, and *ownership through a component the declaring root owns*. Both reopen the link-time collision the orphan rule exists to prevent: two independent packages each declaring `Show({Nat, Nat})` would collide with neither in the wrong, and components are not in the key for the second to reach anyway.
